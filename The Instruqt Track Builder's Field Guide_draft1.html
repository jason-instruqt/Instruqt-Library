Preface

The official Instruqt documentation tells you what the platform does. It tells you that track.yml defines a track, that config.yml declares infrastructure, that check scripts validate learner work. It does not tell you that a stray null byte from a Windows-mounted volume will break instruqt track push with a misleading UTF-8 error, or that fail-message works on containers but silently aborts your check script on a VM, or that the difference between the lighter base image and the cloud-client base image will quietly cost you twenty minutes the first time you reach for aws and it is not there.

Those gaps are where good tracks are won and lost.

This book sits beside the official documentation, not on top of it. The docs are the manual; this is the field guide. It assumes you know what an Instruqt track is and that you have built at least one. From there, it walks the lifecycle of a real production track — discovery, design, scaffold, build, polish, ship — and at every step it tells you what works, what bites, and what experienced authors have stopped doing.

Every pattern in this book is grounded in a track that shipped. Where a pattern is described, it is described concretely enough that you can implement it from the description alone. Where a pitfall is named, the symptom is named too — the actual error message, the actual broken UI, the actual thing you will see at four o'clock on a Friday when you are trying to push for a Monday demo.

Read the book straight through the first time to absorb the lifecycle and the mental model. Come back to single chapters or sections when you have a specific problem in front of you. The book is structured so that any chapter can be read on its own, but the cumulative argument — that good tracks are the product of a small set of repeated, learnable habits — is best understood end to end.

CH 01
Part I — Foundations

What an Instruqt Track Actually Is

A track is the unit of work in Instruqt. It is not a course, not a tutorial, not a page of documentation. It is an environment — real infrastructure, provisioned on demand — paired with a sequence of guided exercises that the learner performs inside that environment. The learner gets a real terminal, a real database, a real cloud account, a real web application. The track tells them what to do with it and verifies that they did it.

That definition is short, but every word matters. Real is the part that distinguishes a track from a screencast or a marketing demo. The infrastructure exists, briefly, just for the learner. They can break it. They can rebuild it. They can do things the author did not anticipate. A well-designed track guides them toward the lesson without removing the option to deviate.

The three-part anatomy

Every track, regardless of complexity, has three parts.

The first is infrastructure — the hosts, networks, cloud accounts, and external services that the learner will interact with. This is declared in config.yml, or skipped in favor of a sandbox preset when one fits. Infrastructure is the substrate of the track; it is what the learner is in.

The second is content — the assignments, the explanations, the screenshots, the quiz questions, the videos. This is what the learner reads. It lives in numbered challenge directories, each with its own assignment.md file containing both YAML frontmatter (configuration) and Markdown body (the human-readable text).

The third is lifecycle scripts — bash scripts that run at specific points in the track's life: when the environment provisions, before each challenge, when the learner clicks Check, when they click Solve, when the track ends. These scripts are the connective tissue between infrastructure and content. They turn declarative YAML into a functioning, testable lab.

A track that gets all three parts right is rare and valuable. Most tracks get one part right — usually content — and the other two suffer for it. This book is largely about giving equal craft to all three.

What a track is not

A track is not a tutorial in the documentation sense. Tutorials are read; tracks are performed. A reader who skims a tutorial gets some value. A learner who skims a track gets none, because the track expects them to type things, run things, fix things. The interactivity is not optional ornamentation; it is the medium.

A track is not a screencast or a demo. A demo is what the seller does to the audience. A track is what the learner does for themselves, with the seller's product in their hands. The transfer of agency is the point.

A track is not a presentation. Presentations move at the speed of the speaker. Tracks move at the speed of the learner — which is to say, slower than the author thinks, with more confusion than the author remembers from when they last looked at the material.

The author's mental model

You are not writing instructions. You are designing an experience that has instructions in it.

The infrastructure must boot reliably and quickly enough that the learner does not lose interest. The assignment must guide without hand-holding. The check scripts must teach when they fail, not just verdict. The polish — the loading messages, the prompts, the layout, the pacing — must feel like someone cared.

When all of those things land, the learner walks away thinking they had a productive hour with the product. They do not think about the track itself. They think about the thing they learned. That is the goal.

Anatomy of a Track Repository

A track lives in a directory. The directory's name is the track's slug, and that slug is the only identifier some Instruqt tooling will use to reference the track. Inside the directory, the layout is conventional and small — most tracks have fewer than a dozen files at the top level — but every file plays a specific role.

The standard layout

track-name/
├── track.yml              Track identity and metadata
├── config.yml             Infrastructure declaration
├── track_scripts/
│   ├── setup-{hostname}   Track-level provisioning, runs before first challenge
│   └── cleanup-{hostname} Track-level teardown
├── 01-first-challenge/
│   ├── assignment.md      Instructions (YAML frontmatter + Markdown body)
│   ├── setup-{hostname}   Per-challenge setup (optional)
│   ├── check-{hostname}   Validation script (exit 0 = pass, exit 1 = fail)
│   ├── solve-{hostname}   Auto-solve script (for testing/skip)
│   └── cleanup-{hostname} Per-challenge cleanup (optional)
├── 02-second-challenge/
│   └── ...
└── assets/                Images and media referenced in assignments

Every item except track.yml is technically optional. A track with only a track.yml and a single challenge directory containing only an assignment.md is a valid track — it just will not do much. Real tracks add infrastructure, lifecycle scripts, and assets in proportion to their ambition.

track.yml as identity

The track.yml file is the track's identity card. It contains the slug, a unique ID assigned by Instruqt, the title, the teaser, the description, the time limit, and a lab_config block that controls UI behavior. Most fields are self-explanatory. A few are worth flagging.

The slug must match the directory name. Instruqt enforces this; mismatches cause push failures.

The id field is a UUID assigned by Instruqt the first time the track is pushed. Do not fabricate one when creating a new track — leave the field out and let the platform assign it. Do not modify the value after it is assigned, because doing so creates an entirely new track in the platform's view.

The checksum field is auto-generated by Instruqt and should never be manually edited. It exists for change detection.

The lab_config block controls layout, theme, sidebar behavior, loading messages, and other UI concerns. These are explored in Chapter 13 and Chapter 14.

config.yml as infrastructure

The config.yml file declares everything the track provisions: containers, virtual machines, virtual browsers, cloud accounts, secrets. It uses a version: "3" schema and supports a small but expressive vocabulary of resource types.

A track that does not need any custom infrastructure can omit config.yml entirely and instead reference a sandbox preset in track.yml. This is rare but valuable when applicable — see Chapter 4 for when to reach for it.

track_scripts/ as the boot sequence

Files inside track_scripts/ run once per lab session, before the first challenge starts. They are conventionally named after the host they run on: setup-workstation, setup-vm-1, cleanup-workstation. The hostname suffix matches the resource name in config.yml.

These scripts are where heavy provisioning happens — installing tools the base image lacks, seeding databases, pre-pulling Docker images, creating users, writing config files. A well-written track-level setup script gets the environment to a "ready for any challenge" state. Per-challenge setup, when present, takes the environment from there to a "ready for this challenge" state.

Numbered challenge directories

Challenge directories use a leading two-digit prefix to enforce ordering: 01-, 02-, 03-. The order must match the sequence in track.yml. Some tracks use a double-number format (01-01-slug-name) for sub-grouped challenges; this is fine as long as the prefix sort order matches the intended sequence.

Each challenge directory contains an assignment.md and optionally its own lifecycle scripts. A challenge that exists only to display content — a quiz, a reading section, a video introduction — needs only assignment.md.

assets/ and supporting files

The assets/ directory holds images, screenshots, and other media referenced in assignments. References are typically relative paths (./assets/screenshot.png) or absolute HTTPS URLs. Some authors also keep build scripts, helper modules, or vendor product files outside the standard layout, in directories named for what they hold (scripts/, lib/, vendor/). These are not platform-recognized but cause no harm.

The repo also benefits from three optional living documents that are not strictly part of the track but make ongoing work materially easier: a README.md (what the track is, for human readers visiting the directory), a CLAUDE.md (conventions and patterns specific to this repository, useful for AI-assisted authoring), and a MEMORY.md (a build log of decisions made, problems hit, and deferred work). None of these are required. All of them pay for themselves over the lifetime of a track.

The Lifecycle: From Idea to Live

A track passes through six recognizable phases on its way from "I should build something to demonstrate this" to "the customer just finished it and gave us positive feedback." The phases are not formal — Instruqt does not enforce them — but they are repeatable enough to be worth naming, and authors who recognize them ship better tracks faster than authors who do not.

The phases are: discovery, design, scaffold, build, test, and ship. This chapter walks each one and names the questions an author should be asking at that phase.

Discovery

Discovery is the phase before any code is written. The author is still deciding what the track is, and the right work at this phase is research and conversation — not YAML.

The questions worth answering during discovery:

  • Who is the learner? A prospect evaluating a product? An existing customer trying to extend their usage? A new hire being onboarded?
  • What does the learner know coming in, and what should they know going out? The delta between those two states is what the track teaches.
  • What is the product surface the track demonstrates? A specific feature? A workflow that touches multiple features? An integration?
  • What infrastructure is the product itself running on, and how realistic does the lab environment need to be?
  • How long should the track take? Forty-five minutes? Two hours? The answer constrains everything downstream.

Discovery often surfaces that the track the author was about to build is not the track the audience needs. That is the most valuable possible outcome of the phase. Better to discover misalignment now than after fifteen hours of build.

Design

Design is where the architecture decisions are made. By the end of design, the author should be able to draw the track on a whiteboard: how many challenges, what each one teaches, what infrastructure they share, what the learner sees in the browser tabs at any given moment.

The design phase is also where the major patterns from this book come into play. Container or VM? Single host or multi-host? Cloud account, and if so, AWS or Azure or GCP? Sandbox preset, or custom infrastructure? Service tab or browser tab for that vendor console? These decisions cascade — the wrong call here means rework everywhere downstream.

A useful design output is a one-page sketch: track title, challenge list with one-line summaries, infrastructure block diagram, and a list of any unfamiliar technologies the author has flagged for further research. If you cannot describe the track in one page, you have not finished designing it.

Scaffold

Scaffolding is the mechanical work of turning the design into a directory structure. Create the track folder. Write a minimal track.yml. Sketch the config.yml. Create the numbered challenge directories with empty assignment.md files containing only frontmatter. Seed a MEMORY.md with the design notes from the previous phase.

Scaffolding is also where many tracks pick up their working name. The slug at this point is provisional — feel free to rename later. The directory's slug, the slug field in track.yml, and the id field will all be reconciled when the track is finalized.

A scaffolded track is not yet a working track. It is a known-shape skeleton ready to be fleshed out.

Build

Build is where most of the work happens. Lifecycle scripts get written. Assignment bodies get drafted. Check scripts get implemented. Solve scripts get written to mirror the check scripts. Tabs and layouts get configured. Branding gets applied. Loading messages get tuned.

The build phase iterates. The author writes a setup script, pushes the track, runs it, finds the script does not work the way they thought, and fixes it. They write a check script, run the challenge, fail it deliberately to see what the learner sees, and refine the failure messages. They notice the assignment is too long, or too short, or assumes too much, and they rewrite.

This iterative quality is what distinguishes a track that ships well from one that ships unhappily. Build is not a phase to be optimized for speed. It is a phase to be optimized for iteration count — how many cycles of "try, observe, refine" the author gets through before pushing for real.

Test

Test is the phase most authors collapse into build. They should not. Build tests challenge by challenge, in the state the author has set up by hand. Test tests the whole track, end to end, in a fresh environment, the way a real learner will encounter it.

The bugs found in test are almost never the bugs found in build. They are bugs of state: challenge two assumed something challenge one set up, and the learner hit the discontinuity even though the author never did. They are bugs of time: the setup script took longer in the real environment than in the author's familiar one, and the learner saw a 502. They are bugs of behavior: the learner did something the author did not anticipate, and the check script gave a misleading message.

Skip test only if you are willing to ship a track you have not actually finished. Most authors are willing more often than they should be.

Ship

Ship is the final phase: a clean push, a sanity check, and a hand-off. The track gets a tag or version label, the platform records the change, and the URL becomes available to the audience.

The ship phase is short, but it has its own pitfalls — push-time validation errors that trace back to Windows-side edits, schema mismatches that surface only in production, the silent shipping of a half-modified track because a previous push had failed and the author thought they had recovered. Chapter 17 catalogs these. Chapter 16 walks the push process itself.

After ship, the track enters maintenance. Vendor products evolve. Cloud APIs change. Loading times drift as base images get heavier. Maintenance is a real phase too, covered in Chapter 18.

When the phases overlap

The phases described above are sequential in principle and overlapping in practice. Most authors discover something halfway through build that sends them back to design. Most tracks acquire a piece of infrastructure during scaffold that turns out to be wrong. The phases are not gates; they are recognizable shapes of work, and naming them helps the author know what kind of work they are doing right now.

The discipline is not "complete every phase before starting the next." The discipline is "do not skip a phase entirely." A track without discovery becomes a solution looking for a problem. A track without design becomes a directory full of half-considered files. A track without test becomes a learner's bad afternoon.

Choosing Your Compute: Container, VM, or Sandbox Preset

The first architectural decision in any track is what kind of machine the learner is talking to. Instruqt offers three compute primitives — containers, virtual machines, and sandbox presets — and the choice between them constrains everything downstream. Boot time, capability, image selection, network exposure options, and even which lifecycle helpers work all flow from this one decision.

Most authors default to whatever they used last time. That is how tracks end up overprovisioned for the work they actually do, slow to start, or unable to host the very tool they were built to demonstrate. This chapter is about choosing on purpose.

The Three Compute Models

A container is a Docker container running an Instruqt-provided base image. It is the lightest and fastest of the three: typical boot time is under thirty seconds, and most lifecycle helpers (fail-message, set-status, the agent variable commands) are built into the standard images. Containers run inside a managed network where the platform handles port routing, certificate provisioning for service tabs, and a handful of conveniences that make exposing applications to the learner's browser nearly free.

A virtual machine is a full Linux VM running either a stock cloud image (Ubuntu LTS, Debian, and the like) or a custom image you have published in a GCP project. VMs boot more slowly — typically a minute or more — and require you to install your own tooling, including the Instruqt agent helpers if you want them. In exchange, you get root access to a real kernel, the ability to run nested virtualization, and the freedom to install anything that needs a real system, including display servers.

A sandbox preset is the platform's escape hatch. Instead of declaring infrastructure in a config.yml file, you point your track.yml at a named, pre-baked environment. There is no boot, no setup script, no provisioning latency the learner experiences — the environment is ready when the track loads. The trade-off is that you are limited to the presets the platform offers and you cannot modify the underlying configuration.

Containers: The Default for Most Work

If your track does not need a kernel, a desktop, or a process that demands one, start with a container.

The two most-used base images are worth knowing by name. The lighter is a minimal shell environment — small, fast, and missing almost everything a cloud-account track will need. The other is a heavier "cloud-client" image with the AWS, Azure, and GCP CLIs pre-installed, plus common scripting tools.

The trap most authors fall into is reaching for the lighter image and then wiring up an aws_accounts block. The container starts, the learner runs aws s3 ls, and the terminal returns "command not found." The fix is either to switch to the cloud-client image or to install the CLI in the track-level setup script before any challenge runs.

Virtual Machines: When You Actually Need One

There are exactly four reasons to choose a VM over a container. Reaching for one without one of these reasons is over-engineering.

The first reason is a display server. Containers in the Instruqt environment do not support X11 or Xvfb. Any application that requires a virtual display — and that includes most browser-based remote desktop solutions built on KasmVNC — has to run inside a VM. Web-based IDE servers do not count: they are web servers that happen to render code, not GUI applications, and they run fine in containers.

The second is kernel-level work. Tracks that demonstrate kernel modules, low-level networking, or systemd unit files generally need a real kernel and systemctl. Containers have neither in any reliable sense.

The third is nested virtualization. If your track demonstrates running VMs inside the lab environment — for example, KVM, libvirt, or a hardware emulator — you need a VM with nested_virtualization: true enabled.

The fourth is a custom image with heavy dependencies. If your track needs a specific build of a vendor product, a database with seeded data, or a multi-gigabyte machine learning model preinstalled, baking it into a custom GCP image and booting from that image is faster and more reliable than installing it during track setup.

A Note on What VMs Do Not Have

Watch out

VMs come without the Instruqt-managed conveniences that containers ship with. The two most painful absences are fail-message and set-status, which are container-only and will exit with status 127 on a VM if you call them. Check scripts that work on a container will silently fail on a VM if they use these helpers, and the platform will display a generic "Something went wrong" modal that points at no specific cause.

This is one of the most expensive debugging surprises in the platform. The cure is to know it exists and to use plain echo plus exit 1 on VM-based tracks. The styled UI hint is lost; the script still works.

Sandbox Presets: The Escape Hatch

When your track demonstrates a vendor platform that the vendor themselves has packaged as an Instruqt preset, use the preset. Skipping config.yml and track_scripts/ entirely removes an entire category of failure modes — boot timing, image pulls, agent installation, environment variable injection — and lets you focus on the assignment content.

The constraint is that you cannot modify the preset. If you need to add a custom dataset, install a third-party CLI, or expose an additional port, you have to switch back to a config.yml-based design.

Pro tip

If the preset is a 90% fit for your track, use it and design around the 10%. If it is a 60% fit, build the environment yourself. The middle ground tends to produce tracks that fight the platform.

A Decision Framework

When standing at this fork, walk down the questions in order:

  1. Does a vendor sandbox preset exist for the product you are demonstrating, and does it cover what you need?
  2. Does your track require a display server, a real kernel, nested virtualization, or a custom image baked with heavy dependencies?
  3. Does the lighter base image have the tooling your assignments require, or do you need the cloud-client image?

The decision is rarely close once the questions are asked in order. The mistakes happen when authors skip the first two and reach straight for whatever they used last time.

Declaring Infrastructure with config.yml

The config.yml file is the schematic for your track's lab environment. It declares every host, every cloud account, every secret, and every external resource the learner will encounter. The file uses version: "3" syntax — a flat YAML document with top-level keys for each resource category.

The Version Directive

Every config.yml begins with a single line declaring the schema version.

version: "3"

Always quote the value. An unquoted 3 is interpreted as an integer and breaks the parser, with an error message that is more confusing than it should be. Quote it and forget it.

Containers

containers:
  - name: workstation
    image: gcr.io/instruqt/cloud-client
    ports: [8080, 5000]
    memory: 1024
    shell: /bin/bash
    environment:
      DATABASE_URL: postgres://app:app@db:5432/app

The name is the hostname the container will be reachable as from other declared resources, and the value lifecycle scripts will use as their host suffix (setup-workstation, check-workstation). Names should be short, descriptive, and lowercase, because they double as DNS labels. Avoid underscores.

ports declares which ports the platform's traffic proxy may route to. A port not listed here cannot be reached from a service tab even if the container is listening on it. memory is in megabytes. shell sets the default shell for terminal tabs. environment is a map of variables to inject into the container.

Virtual Machines

virtualmachines:
  - name: app-server
    image: ubuntu-os-cloud/ubuntu-2204-lts
    memory: 4096
    cpus: 2
    allow_external_ingress: [http, https, high-ports]
    nested_virtualization: false
    provision_ssl_certificate: true

image here points at a cloud image, not a Docker image. Stock public images like ubuntu-os-cloud/ubuntu-2204-lts are available out of the box. Custom images live in the GCP project Instruqt provisions for VM hosting; once published they are referenced as <project-id>/<image-name>.

allow_external_ingress controls which ports are reachable from the public internet. Friendly aliases: http (port 80), https (port 443), high-ports (ports 1024 and above). nested_virtualization enables KVM inside the VM. provision_ssl_certificate triggers the platform to issue a certificate for the VM's external hostname.

Virtual Browsers

virtualbrowsers:
  - name: app-portal
    url: https://app.${_SANDBOX_ID}.instruqt.io/

The url field supports interpolation: ${_SANDBOX_ID} resolves to the unique session ID, useful for URLs that change per session.

Cloud Account Blocks

aws_accounts:
  - name: lab
    services: [s3, ec2, iam]
    regions: [us-east-1]
    managed_policies:
      - arn:aws:iam::aws:policy/AmazonS3ReadOnlyAccess
    admin_managed_policies:
      - arn:aws:iam::aws:policy/AdministratorAccess

Permissions are declared via managed_policies / admin_managed_policies (AWS, by ARN), roles / admin_roles (GCP and Azure), or inline iam_policy / admin_iam_policy (AWS, JSON form). Chapter 6 covers identity and permissions in depth.

Secrets

secrets:
  - name: VENDOR_API_KEY
  - name: ARANGO_PWD

Use this for credentials, API tokens, license keys, and anything sensitive that should not live in version control. Values are configured per track in the platform and become available as ${VENDOR_API_KEY} inside containers and VMs.

Putting It Together

version: "3"

containers:
  - name: workstation
    image: gcr.io/instruqt/cloud-client
    ports: [5000]
    memory: 1024
    shell: /bin/bash
    environment:
      DATABASE_URL: postgres://app:app@db:5432/app

  - name: db
    image: postgres:16
    ports: [5432]
    memory: 1024
    environment:
      POSTGRES_USER: app
      POSTGRES_PASSWORD: app
      POSTGRES_DB: app

secrets:
  - name: VENDOR_API_KEY

A config.yml is a contract between the author and every future reader of the track. Keep it short. Trim is a feature.

Identity, Permissions, and Cloud Accounts

Cloud-account tracks add a dimension that container-only tracks do not have: identity. The learner is no longer just a user on a Linux host; they are a principal in a cloud account, with a specific set of permissions, and the difference between "the lab works" and "the lab works correctly" often comes down to whether those permissions match the assignment.

The Two-Account Model

Every Instruqt cloud account block — aws_accounts, azure_subscriptions, gcp_projects — provisions two identities, not one.

The user identity is the learner. It has the permissions the track grants for the assignment, and only those permissions. If the assignment is "list the S3 buckets," the user has s3:ListAllMyBuckets. It does not have permission to delete buckets, modify IAM, or do anything outside the assignment's scope.

The admin identity is the track itself. It has elevated permissions used by lifecycle scripts to provision resources, seed data, validate state, and clean up. Admin operations happen invisibly to the learner; admin credentials are exposed to scripts via auto-injected environment variables but are not surfaced in the learner's terminal sessions or browser tabs.

User does what the lab teaches; admin does what the lab needs to function.

AWS IAM Patterns

AWS account blocks support four permission-declaration mechanisms: managed_policies (user policies referenced by ARN), admin_managed_policies (admin policies by ARN), iam_policy (inline user policy as JSON), and admin_iam_policy (inline admin policy as JSON). A fifth field, scp_policy, applies a Service Control Policy to the account for organization-level restrictions.

For more constrained tracks — say, "create exactly one EC2 instance with these tags" — an inline policy is often clearer than chaining managed policies:

iam_policy: |
  {
    "Version": "2012-10-17",
    "Statement": [{
      "Effect": "Allow",
      "Action": ["ec2:RunInstances", "ec2:DescribeInstances"],
      "Resource": "*",
      "Condition": {
        "StringEquals": { "aws:RequestTag/Track": "ec2-basics" }
      }
    }]
  }

Inline policies are heavier to write but precise. They are the right tool when no managed policy fits the constraint cleanly.

Azure Roles and admin_roles

azure_subscriptions:
  - name: lab
    services: [Microsoft.Compute, Microsoft.Storage]
    regions: [eastus]
    roles: [Reader]
    admin_roles: [Owner]

The role names are the standard Azure RBAC roles — Reader, Contributor, Owner — plus any custom roles defined in the subscription.

GCP Roles and Service Accounts

gcp_projects:
  - name: lab
    services:
      - compute.googleapis.com
      - container.googleapis.com
    regions: [us-central1]
    roles: [roles/compute.viewer]
    admin_roles: [roles/owner]

Two patterns are worth flagging on GCP specifically. The first is the service account key dichotomy: the platform provisions both a user-scoped service account key (SERVICE_ACCOUNT_KEY) and an admin service account key (ADMIN_SERVICE_ACCOUNT_KEY). The second is the auth credential override — setting CLOUDSDK_AUTH_CREDENTIAL_FILE_OVERRIDE to the admin key file activates admin credentials only for the duration of a single command, leaving the learner's gcloud session unchanged.

CLOUDSDK_AUTH_CREDENTIAL_FILE_OVERRIDE=$ADMIN_SERVICE_ACCOUNT_KEY \
  gcloud projects add-iam-policy-binding "$PROJECT_ID" \
  --member="user:$USER_EMAIL" --role=roles/compute.admin

Auto-Injected Credentials

The platform injects per-account credentials as environment variables for every cloud block declared. The naming pattern is INSTRUQT_{CLOUD}_{ACCOUNT_TYPE}_{NAME}_{FIELD}, where {NAME} is the cloud account block's name, uppercased and with hyphens replaced by underscores. The full table for all three clouds appears in Appendix B.

Common Permission Failures

Three failure modes recur often enough to flag.

The first is missing service enablement on AWS or GCP. The user has the right policy or role, but the underlying API is not enabled, and the first call fails with "service not enabled" or "API has not been used." The fix is to add the service to the services array.

The second is insufficient admin permissions when a setup script needs to provision resources. The track works on the author's machine because the author's local CLI is logged in as a project owner. In the lab, the admin role is too narrow.

The third is admin-as-user pollution. A setup script forgets to scope admin operations and runs them in the learner's default credential context. By the time the learner opens a terminal, their cloud CLI is authenticated as the admin, and any check script that validates "the user can do X but cannot do Y" produces false passes because the user is implicitly the admin.

Exposing Applications to the Learner's Browser

A track is rarely just a terminal. Most production tracks pair the shell with one or more browser-rendered surfaces: a web UI, a database admin console, a graphical IDE, a cloud provider's console, a desktop application running remotely. The surface area of "things in a browser" is one of the largest in the platform, and the tab type system is how you choose what the learner sees.

The Tab Type Spectrum

Instruqt offers six tab types in the assignment frontmatter, and they break naturally into three groups by what they connect to.

The internal group connects to applications running on hosts you have declared in config.yml: terminal (a shell on a container or VM) and service (a web UI served from a container or VM port, routed through Instruqt's traffic proxy).

The content group displays content the learner authors or reads: code (a file editor pointed at a path on a host) and browser (a cloud console browser tied to a virtualbrowsers block).

The external group renders something that is not part of your track's infrastructure at all: external (any URL in an embedded frame) and website (an external URL tab, with the strict requirement that the URL must be HTTPS).

Service Tabs and the Proxy Rule

The service tab is the most powerful of the six and the most commonly misused. A service tab takes a hostname (matching one of your declared containers or VMs) and a port, and routes traffic to that port through Instruqt's managed proxy.

The proxy rule

The learner's browser cannot resolve internal Docker hostnames. If your service tab points at hostname workstation on port 8080, and the page contains JavaScript that calls fetch('http://api:5000/data'), that fetch will fail. The browser is on the learner's laptop, talking to the Instruqt proxy — not inside your Docker network.

The fix is to use relative paths only in any client-side code your service tab serves. fetch('/api/data') works because the call routes back through the same proxy endpoint. fetch('http://api:5000/data') does not, and no amount of CORS configuration will rescue it.

Browser Tabs versus Service Tabs

The browser tab and the service tab look similar at a glance, but they connect to different things. A browser tab renders a virtualbrowsers resource — typically the cloud console for an AWS, Azure, or GCP account that the platform has provisioned. A service tab renders an HTTP server on a host you declared.

Graphical Applications in the Browser

For graphical applications that do not have a web mode of their own, the browser-rendered options break into three patterns.

The first is a web-based VS Code. There is a server distribution of the editor that runs as an HTTP service on a port. Install it in your VM setup, expose it via a service tab, and the learner has a full editor in a browser tab with no extra plumbing.

The second is KasmVNC — a Docker image family that wraps a single GUI application and exposes it over WebVNC on an HTTP port. You run the KasmVNC container on a VM, expose port 8080 via a service tab, and the learner sees the application in a tab.

The third is noVNC plus a real desktop. Install a window manager and a VNC server on a VM, then layer noVNC on top to bridge VNC to WebSocket. Expose via a website tab using the sandbox-aware URL.

A fourth option, Guacamole, exists for RDP into a Windows VM, but it is a heavier setup with its own configuration file and a poll-for-readiness pattern that has to be wired into the VM setup script.

Private Network Exposure

When your track provisions a cloud VM that should not have a public IP, you cannot expose its ports through a service tab directly, because the service tab needs the hostname to be reachable from your declared infrastructure.

The answer is an IAP tunnel: from a workstation container, open a tunnel to the private VM and map remote ports to local ports. The terminal tab then SSHes into localhost on the tunnel port; the service tab points at localhost on a different tunnel port for the web UI.

Choosing Between Patterns

  1. Is the surface code or text the learner is editing? Use a code tab.
  2. Is the surface a web UI served by your own infrastructure? Use a service tab.
  3. Is the surface external — a vendor console, a public site, or a cloud provider console? Use browser, external, or website, depending on whether it ties to a declared resource and whether the platform should manage authentication.

Lifecycle Scripts: Setup, Check, Solve, Cleanup

Lifecycle scripts are the bash files that run at specific points in a track's life. There are four conventional types — setup, check, solve, cleanup — and each runs at a different moment, with a different responsibility. Most of a track's behavior beyond the YAML and Markdown lives in these scripts.

The Four Script Types

A setup script runs before its scope becomes active. A track-level setup runs once per session, before the first challenge. A challenge-level setup runs before that specific challenge becomes active. Setup scripts provision: install tools, seed databases, write config files, create users, start services, pre-pull Docker images.

A check script runs when the learner clicks the Check button. Its job is to validate that the learner has done the work the assignment described, and to teach when they have not. Exit code zero means pass; exit code one means fail.

A solve script runs when the learner (or the author, during testing) clicks the Solve button. Its job is to put the host into the exact state the corresponding check script will accept as a pass.

A cleanup script runs when its scope ends. Most tracks do not need cleanup scripts at all because the platform tears down the entire environment between sessions. Cleanup scripts earn their place when a track touches resources outside the lab — external SaaS accounts, remote APIs, persistent state — that the platform cannot clean up automatically.

Naming and Hostname Suffixes

Script filenames encode the host they run on. A script named setup-workstation runs on the host named workstation; a script named check-db runs on the host named db. The platform parses the suffix and dispatches accordingly. Misnaming a script — or naming it without a hostname suffix — silently disables it.

The Bootstrap Wait

Track-level setup scripts run early in the lab's life, often before all hosts have finished provisioning. The defensive pattern is to wait for a known bootstrap marker before doing anything else:

#!/bin/bash
set -euxo pipefail

until [ -f /opt/instruqt/bootstrap/host-bootstrap-completed ]; do
  sleep 1
done

# … rest of setup …

The marker file is placed by the platform itself when the host is ready for user-mode scripts to run. Without this wait, setup scripts intermittently fail in ways that are difficult to reproduce.

set -euo pipefail and Its Traps

Every lifecycle script should start with set -euo pipefail (or set -euxo pipefail for setup scripts where verbose tracing is helpful). Together they make scripts fail loudly and early. The trap to know about: any unguarded subprocess call that exits non-zero will trigger an immediate, silent bash exit, and Instruqt renders a generic "Something went wrong" modal.

RESULT=$(curl -fsS "$API/healthz") || {
  echo "API health check failed."
  exit 1
}

Helper Modules

For tracks with many check or solve scripts that share validation logic, write the shared logic once and import it from each script. The cleanest pattern is a Python module installed in the standard dist-packages location during track setup:

# In track_scripts/setup-workstation
cat > /usr/lib/python3/dist-packages/instruqt_tests.py <<'EOF'
import subprocess

def test_command(cmd, expected_rc=0):
    return subprocess.run(cmd, shell=True, capture_output=True).returncode == expected_rc

def test_file_exists(path):
    import os
    return os.path.isfile(path)

def test_string_in_file(path, needle):
    return needle in open(path).read()
EOF

set-workdir

The set-workdir helper sets the default working directory for terminal tabs on a host. Use it in a setup script to drop the learner into a sensible directory rather than /root:

set-workdir /workspace

Cross-Host State with agent variable

When a setup script on host A produces a value that a check script on host B needs to read, agent variable is the bridge.

# On host A's setup
DB_ENDPOINT=$(aws rds describe-db-instances ... --query '...' --output text)
agent variable set DB_ENDPOINT "$DB_ENDPOINT"

# On host B's check
DB_ENDPOINT=$(agent variable get DB_ENDPOINT)
psql "host=$DB_ENDPOINT ..."

Cleanup Strategy

Most tracks need no cleanup. The exceptions are tracks that touch state outside the lab. A cleanup script is the right place to undo external state. Wrap the cleanup logic defensively:

#!/bin/bash
set -uo pipefail

curl -X DELETE "$API/customers/$INSTRUQT_PARTICIPANT_ID" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $API_TOKEN" \
  || echo "Cleanup failed for customer $INSTRUQT_PARTICIPANT_ID — review manually."

Note the -uo pipefail without -e: in cleanup, you generally want to continue past individual failures rather than bail.

The script can be wrong and the learner will still figure it out from the assignment. The assignment can be wrong and no amount of scripting will rescue them.

The assignment body is the closest thing in a track to "the teacher talking to the learner." It carries the voice. Every other element of the track — the infrastructure, the scripts, the tabs — is invisible if the body is well-written, because the learner never has to think about them. They just do the work.

Address the learner directly — second person ("you"), present tense.

Move at the learner's speed. Each step should be small enough that a beginner can complete it without stopping.

Show what success looks like. After every action, the learner should have a clear sense of what they should see.

Resist the temptation to explain everything. A track is not a textbook.

Variables, Secrets, and State Between Hosts

A track is a multi-host system. Containers and VMs each have their own filesystem, their own environment, their own running processes. When a setup script on one host needs to communicate a value to a check script on another, or when an assignment body needs to display a value the platform generated dynamically, the platform's variable mechanisms are the bridge.

Auto-Injected Environment Variables

Every host in a track receives a set of environment variables injected by the platform at provisioning time. The most universally available is INSTRUQT_PARTICIPANT_ID — a unique ID for the current lab session.

Cloud-specific variables follow a pattern: INSTRUQT_{CLOUD}_{ACCOUNT_TYPE}_{NAME}_{FIELD}. Appendix B is the full reference.

echo "Provisioning resources for $INSTRUQT_PARTICIPANT_ID"
aws s3 mb "s3://demo-$INSTRUQT_PARTICIPANT_ID"

Secrets

secrets:
  - name: VENDOR_API_KEY
  - name: ARANGO_PWD

In assignment bodies, secrets cannot be interpolated directly — that would expose them to the learner, defeating the point. Treat secrets as values the script knows about and the learner does not, unless the lesson is specifically about handling secrets.

agent variable: State Between Hosts and Stages

The agent variable mechanism is the platform's general-purpose key/value store, scoped to the lab session.

# Set
agent variable set DB_ENDPOINT "myhost.example.com:5432"

# Read
DB_ENDPOINT=$(agent variable get DB_ENDPOINT)

The variable is available from any host in the track and persists across challenges. It is the right tool for values that:

  • Are generated at provisioning time and need to be referenced later.
  • Are produced by a setup script and consumed by a check or solve script on a different host.
  • Need to be shown to the learner in an assignment body via Instruqt-Var.

Cross-Host Coordination Patterns

Three patterns recur often enough to name.

The first is provisioning hand-off. Host A's setup script provisions an external resource and writes the resource's identifier to an agent variable. Host B's check script reads the variable and validates against the resource. Host C's cleanup script reads the variable and tears the resource down.

The second is dynamic credential propagation. The track-level setup script generates a random password, stores it in agent variable, and configures every relevant service to use it.

The third is multi-challenge state. A challenge in early in the track produces a value (a customer ID, a deployment URL, an analytics token). Later challenges read the value from agent variable rather than re-creating it.

Privileges and Visibility

Auto-injected variables and agent variable values are visible to any process running on the host. That includes the learner's terminal, processes the learner starts, and any web service the learner can reach. Treat anything in these as "the learner can see this if they look."

Secrets are also visible to processes on the host. They are not invisible to the learner — they are simply not advertised. If a track genuinely needs to hide a value from the learner, the value cannot live on a host the learner has access to.

Check Scripts That Teach

A check script's job is not to determine pass or fail. Its job is to teach the learner what they got wrong and how to fix it.

This is a real distinction, and most check scripts ignore it. The default pattern — a single bash script that performs every assertion in one chain, exits 1 on the first failure, and emits a generic message — does the bare minimum the platform requires. It satisfies the contract. It does not help the learner.

The Lazy Pattern

#!/bin/bash
set -euo pipefail

# Validate user setup
sudo -u postgres psql -tAc "SELECT 1 FROM pg_roles WHERE rolname='analyst'" \
  | grep -q 1 \
  && sudo -u postgres psql -tAc "SELECT has_table_privilege('analyst', 'orders', 'SELECT')" \
  | grep -q t \
  && sudo -u postgres psql -tAc "SELECT has_table_privilege('analyst', 'orders', 'DELETE')" \
  | grep -q f

if [ $? -ne 0 ]; then
  echo "User is not configured correctly"
  exit 1
fi

When this fails, the learner sees "User is not configured correctly." That message tells them nothing. They will re-read the assignment, run the same SQL again, possibly start over. This is where learners abandon tracks.

The Granular Pattern

#!/bin/bash
set -euo pipefail

# 1. User exists
if ! sudo -u postgres psql -tAc \
     "SELECT 1 FROM pg_roles WHERE rolname='analyst'" | grep -q 1; then
  fail-message "User 'analyst' does not exist. Run: CREATE USER analyst WITH PASSWORD 'changeme';"
  exit 1
fi

# 2. User is not a superuser
if sudo -u postgres psql -tAc \
   "SELECT rolsuper FROM pg_roles WHERE rolname='analyst'" | grep -q t; then
  fail-message "User 'analyst' is a superuser. Run: ALTER USER analyst NOSUPERUSER;"
  exit 1
fi

# 3. SELECT on orders
if ! sudo -u postgres psql -tAc \
     "SELECT has_table_privilege('analyst', 'orders', 'SELECT')" | grep -q t; then
  fail-message "User 'analyst' lacks SELECT on orders. Run: GRANT SELECT ON orders TO analyst;"
  exit 1
fi

# 4. No DELETE on orders
if sudo -u postgres psql -tAc \
   "SELECT has_table_privilege('analyst', 'orders', 'DELETE')" | grep -q t; then
  fail-message "User 'analyst' has DELETE on orders. Run: REVOKE DELETE ON orders FROM analyst;"
  exit 1
fi

set-status "Permissions configured correctly."
exit 0

Same logical assertions. Wildly different learner experience. Each failure now points at one specific thing and gives the learner the exact command to run. The Check button stops being a binary verdict and becomes a teaching tool.

The fail-message Trap

Warning

fail-message and set-status are container-only. On a VM-based track they will fail with exit status 127 and Instruqt will render the generic "Something went wrong" modal — not because your script crashed, but because the helper binary does not exist in the VM's path. This is one of the most expensive debugging traps in the platform, because the modal blames the script and not the missing helper.

On VM-based tracks, fall back to echo for failure messages and rely on plain exit 1. You lose the styled UI hint, but the message still surfaces in the Instruqt debug log and you avoid a mystery.

The set -euo pipefail Trap

set -euo pipefail at the top of a check script is good defensive hygiene — until you start invoking subprocesses. Any unguarded python3 -c ... or curl ... or psql ... that exits non-zero will trigger an immediate, silent bash exit.

RESULT=$(curl -fsS "$API/healthz") || {
  fail-message "API health check failed. Is the service running?"
  exit 1
}

Validating Intent, Not Intermediate State

When a resource progresses through multiple statuses — ingested → classified → enriched → governed, or pending → running → succeeded → archived — a later operation may overwrite an earlier status. A check script that asserts a specific intermediate state will produce false negatives on resources that have legitimately advanced further.

case "$STATUS" in
  enriched|governed)
    # both indicate enrichment occurred
    ;;
  *)
    fail-message "Resource has not been enriched yet."
    exit 1
    ;;
esac

The general form: a check script's pass condition is a property of the resource, not a position in a state machine. Express it as the property.

Solve Scripts as Mirrors

A solve script's relationship to its check script is one of mirroring: the solve produces exactly the state the check accepts as a pass.

This is harder than it looks. A solve script that creates a user but forgets to grant SELECT will pass informally on the author's machine and fail the actual check, often after a long debugging session.

The discipline is to read the check script first, enumerate every property it tests, and write a solve script that explicitly establishes every one of those properties — no more, no less. Idempotency matters: the solve script must succeed when run twice. Completeness matters: it must establish every property the check looks for.

Branding the Experience

A track is a product. Like any product, it can feel deliberate or generic. The difference between the two is rarely about content — it is about the small surfaces the author chose to brand: the shell prompt, the CLI commands, the portal page, the loading messages, the choice of theme.

The Shell Prompt

The default bash prompt — root@hostname:/path# — announces every detail of the learner's environment whether they want it or not. A custom PS1 in the user's .bashrc replaces the default with something quieter and on-brand:

# In track_scripts/setup-workstation
echo "export PS1='acme:\w\$ '" >> /home/iggy/.bashrc

The learner now sees acme:/workspace$ instead of root@workstation:/workspace#. The information density is identical; the cognitive load is much lower.

The Non-Root User Pattern

# In track_scripts/setup-workstation
useradd -m -s /bin/bash iggy

# Copy or symlink dotfiles so env vars work in the user's session
cp /root/.bashrc /home/iggy/.bashrc
cp /root/.psqlrc /home/iggy/.psqlrc 2>/dev/null || true
chown iggy:iggy /home/iggy/.bashrc /home/iggy/.psqlrc 2>/dev/null || true

Then in the assignment frontmatter:

tabs:
  - title: Terminal
    type: terminal
    hostname: workstation
    cmd: su - iggy

Profile.d Plus .bashrc Dual-Write

A subtle wrinkle: env vars exported in .bashrc are available to interactive non-login shells. Env vars exported in /etc/profile.d/*.sh are available to login shells (which is what su - iggy produces). If your track depends on env vars being available in both contexts, write them to both places.

# /etc/profile.d/acme.sh (login shells via su -)
cat > /etc/profile.d/acme.sh <<'EOF'
export ACME_API_BASE="http://localhost:8080"
export PS1='acme:\w$ '
EOF
chmod +x /etc/profile.d/acme.sh

# /home/iggy/.bashrc (non-login interactive shells)
cat >> /home/iggy/.bashrc <<'EOF'
export ACME_API_BASE="http://localhost:8080"
export PS1='acme:\w$ '
EOF

The redundancy looks ugly. It works. Without it, env vars surface in some terminal sessions and not others, and you spend an afternoon debugging why.

Custom CLI Wrappers

If your track teaches a product that exposes a REST API, consider building a small custom CLI wrapper that the learner uses instead of (or alongside) raw curl commands. A Python script using httpx for HTTP and rich for tables, panels, and colored output produces a CLI that feels like the product's own tooling.

Branded Portal Pages

For tracks that expose a web UI as a service tab, the visual identity of the page is the visual identity of the lab. A page styled to match the product being taught — same colors, same fonts, same logo — feels like part of the product.

The Polish Threshold

The threshold to aim for is “the learner does not notice the lab at all” — not because the lab is invisible, but because the lab feels like part of the product they are learning.

If you find yourself adding polish for the sake of polish, you have crossed it.

Loading, Pausing, and Time

Time is the constraint the learner feels most. A track that takes longer than it advertised feels slow. A track that ends before they finish feels rushed. A track that loads with no indication of what is happening feels broken.

timelimit and the Track-Level Clock

The timelimit field in track.yml sets the total time the learner has to complete the track, in seconds.

timelimit: 3600   # one hour

The right value is the longest a learner is likely to need plus a 25–50% margin. Tracks that are too short pressure the learner; tracks that are too long invite distraction.

show_timer

The show_timer field controls whether the running clock is visible to the learner. For most tracks, leaving the timer visible is the right call. For tracks where the time pressure would be counterproductive, hiding the timer keeps the experience meditative.

skipping_enabled

The skipping_enabled field controls whether the learner can skip past a challenge they cannot complete. With it on, a stuck learner can move forward and continue learning rather than abandoning the track. Without it, every learner who reaches the end has actually completed every challenge — useful for assessments and certifications.

idle_timeout

idle_timeout: 1800   # 30 minutes

Idle timeout balances cost against learner experience. Too short and the learner who steps away for coffee comes back to a torn-down lab. Too long and the platform pays for resources nobody is using.

pausable and pausable_ttl

pausable: true
pausable_ttl: 604800   # 7 days

Pausable tracks change the learner's relationship with time. They become a thing the learner picks up across multiple sittings, like a course rather than a workshop.

Testing Your Track End to End

Testing a track is the work most authors do least and most regret skipping. The platform makes it easy to push a single challenge, run that challenge, fix what's broken, and push again. It does not make it easy to run the whole track in a fresh environment, the way a real learner will encounter it. That is the test that matters, and that is the test that catches the bugs the author never sees.

Why Challenge-by-Challenge Is Insufficient

Authors who push frequently and test challenge by challenge eventually ship a track that works for every individual challenge but fails when run end to end. The cause is almost always state: challenge two assumes something challenge one set up, and the learner hits the discontinuity even though the author never did, because the author tested challenge two starting from a state they manually established.

The Fresh-Environment Test

The single highest-value test you can run on a track is to start a fresh lab session and click through every challenge in order, from start to finish, without manual intervention between challenges.

Use the Solve button on each challenge if you do not want to type out every command yourself. The point is not to validate the assignments — you have already done that. The point is to validate that the track's state machine works when nothing is set up by hand.

Testing Solve Scripts

A solve script's job is to put the host into the exact state the corresponding check script accepts as a pass. Two properties matter: idempotency and completeness.

To test idempotency, run the solve script twice in a fresh environment, then run the check. If both runs of the solve succeed and the check passes, the script is idempotent.

To test completeness, read the check script, enumerate every property it tests, and verify the solve script establishes every one. Off-by-one is the most common solve script bug.

Smoke-Testing Checks

Check scripts have their own testing rhythm. After writing a check, deliberately fail it — do something the check is supposed to catch, run the check, and see what the learner sees. Is the failure message specific enough to act on? Does it name the right command? Does it surface in the UI without truncation?

Edge Cases

Beyond the happy path, three edge cases are worth deliberate testing.

The first is the skip button. If skipping_enabled is true, a learner can skip past any challenge. Test the track with the skip button used at challenge two — does challenge three still work?

The second is timeout. What happens if the learner runs out of time mid-challenge? The lab tears down. State is lost. Cleanup runs (or does not).

The third is failure recovery. What happens if a setup script fails midway through? Most tracks have no recovery story; the learner sees a broken environment and starts over.

The Test Plan in Practice

  1. Push the latest version of the track to a non-production version.
  2. Start a fresh lab session.
  3. Run every solve script in order, one challenge at a time, without manual setup between challenges.
  4. Run every check script after its solve. Verify pass.
  5. Deliberately fail each check at least once. Verify the failure message is useful.
  6. If skipping is enabled, run a session where you skip at least one challenge. Verify subsequent challenges still work.
  7. If the track has external state (SaaS resources, remote APIs), verify that cleanup actually cleans them up.

Pushing, Versioning, and the checksum Field

A track lives in two places: the local repository where the author edits it, and the Instruqt platform where learners run it. The bridge between them is the instruqt track push command.

The instruqt track push Command

cd /path/to/track
instruqt track push

The command reads track.yml, walks the challenge directories, validates the structure, computes a checksum, and uploads everything. On success, the platform's record of the track is updated and the track becomes available at its production URL.

What Happens During a Push

The push process is roughly: validation, packaging, upload, server-side validation, deployment.

Local validation runs first. The CLI checks that track.yml is well-formed YAML, that the slug matches the directory name, that referenced files exist, that challenge directories are correctly numbered, that assignment.md files have valid frontmatter.

Packaging zips the track's files and computes a checksum.

Upload sends the package to the platform.

Server-side validation runs more thorough checks: that all id fields are valid UUIDs, that the schema is current, that tab references resolve, that website URLs are HTTPS.

Deployment commits the new version to the platform's record.

The id Field

Every track and every challenge has an id field — a UUID that the platform uses as the canonical identifier. The id survives slug changes, file moves, and renames; the slug does not.

When creating a new track or challenge, omit the id field. The platform assigns one on first push. Once assigned, do not modify it.

The checksum Field

Rule

Leave the checksum alone. If the field is wrong or missing, the next push fixes it. Modifying it manually causes intermittent strange behavior — the platform sees the local file as inconsistent with its record, and either rejects pushes or overwrites the local value silently.

Production vs. Development Versions

Most tracks have at least two versions on the platform: the production version that learners run, and a development version the author uses for testing. The development version is typically a separate "track" record on the platform, often with a -dev suffix in its slug.

Track Versions and History

The platform retains a history of every push for a given track. Older versions remain accessible from the platform UI, and learners who started a session before a recent push complete that session against the version they started on. This means that pushing a fix during a live session does not break learners already in flight.

What Push Does Not Do

A push does not run the track. A push that succeeds means the platform accepted the upload, not that the track works. The fresh-environment test is the only thing that proves the track works.

A push does not validate that the track makes pedagogical sense. The platform checks structure, syntax, and referential integrity. It cannot tell whether the assignment teaches what it intends to teach or whether the check scripts catch the cases they should. That is human work.

Common Failures at Push Time

The instruqt track push command surfaces a handful of failures often enough that recognizing them by sight saves real time.

The UTF-8 / Null Byte Error

The single most common push failure on Windows-host repositories. The error message reads:

invalid byte sequence for encoding "UTF8": 0x00

The error names UTF-8, but the actual cause is stray null bytes (0x00) introduced by Windows-mounted filesystems during editing.

import pathlib
for p in pathlib.Path(".").rglob("*"):
    if p.suffix in (".yml", ".md") and p.is_file():
        b = p.read_bytes()
        if b'\x00' in b:
            p.write_bytes(b.replace(b'\x00', b''))
            print(f"stripped: {p}")

Run after every editing session on a Windows host. The fix takes seconds; chasing the bogus UTF-8 error without recognizing it can take a quarter of an afternoon.

Schema Validation Errors

  • Unknown field: a typo or a deprecated field name. The error message names the offending field. Compare against the documented schema; correct or remove.
  • Missing required field: usually slug, title, or id. Add it.
  • Wrong type: a field that should be a list given as a string, or vice versa. The error names the type mismatch. Adjust.
  • Invalid enum value: a field with a constrained value set (type, theme.name) given an unrecognized value. The error usually lists the valid options. Pick one.

HTTPS Validation on website Tabs

website tab url must be HTTPS: http://example.com

The platform does not negotiate. The fix is to use an HTTPS URL — switch to a service tab if the destination genuinely cannot serve HTTPS, or use an external tab which is more permissive.

Slug Mismatches

slug mismatch: track.yml says "my-track" but directory is "my-other-track"

Fix: rename the directory or update the slug field. The two have to agree.

Missing or Duplicate IDs

duplicate id: 4f3c9e5a-... appears in challenges 03-foo and 04-foo-copy

Fix: delete the id field from the new challenge before pushing.

Recovery Strategies

  1. Read the error message carefully. The failure mode is almost always named, even if the cause is not.
  2. Check the obvious: null bytes (run the strip script), HTTPS on website tabs, slug mismatches, missing IDs.
  3. If a recent change caused the failure, undo just that change and try again. Bisect the failure if the change is large.
  4. If the failure is transient (network, auth), retry after a short wait.
  5. If nothing else works, push a known-good version of the track to confirm the platform is operational, then re-introduce changes one piece at a time.

Maintaining Tracks Over Time

A track that ships is not a track that is finished. Vendor products evolve. Cloud APIs change. Base images get heavier. Browser behaviors shift. Loading times drift. The track that worked perfectly six months ago will, eventually, stop working — sometimes catastrophically (a vendor's API endpoint is removed), sometimes subtly (a learner reports the lab is "slow now"). Maintenance is the phase of a track's life that nobody designs for and everybody pays.

When to Refactor

The instinct to refactor a track is mostly correct, and mostly poorly timed. The right moments to refactor are usually:

  • A vendor product update introduces a breaking change in the API the track demonstrates.
  • A common maintenance task has been done three or more times across the track and could be centralized into a helper.
  • The track has accumulated dead branches — challenges that were once relevant but no longer are.
  • An author who is not the original author has read the track and gotten lost.

The wrong moment to refactor is "I have an afternoon free and the track looks messy." Cosmetic refactors of working tracks rarely improve them and frequently introduce regressions.

Updating Dependencies

Tracks depend on a stack of things that change at different rates: the Instruqt platform itself, base images, vendor product versions, third-party libraries, cloud APIs.

Vendor product updates are the most common source of track drift. A track that demonstrates a product whose UI changed or whose API endpoint moved will start failing for new learners with no obvious cause. Subscribe to the vendor's release notes if the track is important enough to maintain proactively.

Handling Vendor Product Changes

Update the track to match the new product version. This is the right answer when the change is small and the existing track structure still makes sense.

Branch the track for both versions. Maintain track-name-v2 alongside track-name-v1 if both versions of the product are in active customer use.

Deprecate and replace. When the product change is large enough that the track's premise no longer makes sense, retire the old track and build a new one. Preserve the old version's URL with a notice pointing learners at the replacement.

Track Memory as a Living Document

A MEMORY.md at the track-level — one file per track, alongside track.yml — pays for itself across maintenance sessions. The skeleton is small:

# MEMORY — track-name

## Session History

### YYYY-MM-DD — short note
- What was done
- What was decided
- What broke and how it was fixed

## Deferred Work

- Things noticed during build that did not block ship
- Things that broke and were patched but should be properly fixed later

## Track Architecture Notes

- Non-obvious choices that future maintainers should know about
- Image versions, env vars, gotchas specific to this track

The Audit Cadence

  • Boot times that have crept up.
  • Loading messages that no longer match the track.
  • Vendor product UI changes the track has not absorbed.
  • Documentation links in the assignment that have rotted.
  • Cloud regions that are no longer best-practice.
  • Helper scripts that depend on tools that have evolved.

Knowing When to Sunset

Some tracks should be retired. The product they demonstrate has been deprecated. The audience they targeted has moved on. The pedagogical approach they embody has been superseded by something better.

Sunsetting a track is unglamorous but valuable. A retired track frees its slot in the catalog, signals to the audience that the team is curating actively, and removes a maintenance burden that had been costing real time.

Pattern Catalog

This appendix indexes the architectural patterns used in production tracks. Each pattern is named and described in one line.

Infrastructure patterns

  • Single container — one Docker container, no other infrastructure. The simplest possible track shape.
  • Single VM — one virtual machine, used when the track needs a real kernel, display server, or custom image.
  • Multi-container — two or more containers wired together by environment variables.
  • Multi-VM — two or more VMs, used for tracks that demonstrate distributed systems.
  • Container plus VM — a container as the workstation, a VM as the host being managed.
  • Container plus Postgres plus dashboard — workstation container, postgres container, web dashboard served from the workstation.
  • Container plus FastAPI service — workstation plus an API container, with the FastAPI Swagger UI exposed as a service tab.
  • Container plus mock REST API plus custom CLI — workstation runs a custom branded CLI; a co-located container hosts a mock REST API the CLI talks to.
  • Container plus AWS account — workstation container plus a provisioned AWS account.
  • Container plus Azure subscription — workstation container plus a provisioned Azure subscription.
  • Container plus GCP project — workstation container plus a provisioned GCP project.
  • Container plus GCP project with IAP tunnel — private GCP VM exposed via IAP tunnel from the workstation container.
  • VM plus virtual browser — VM hosts the application; a virtual browser tab points at it.
  • VM plus Guacamole — Windows or Linux desktop accessed via Guacamole RDP/VNC.
  • VM plus noVNC desktop — Linux desktop in a browser tab via noVNC and a window manager.
  • VM plus KasmVNC — single GUI application in a browser tab via KasmVNC on a VM.
  • VM plus Docker plus ttyd — VM running Docker containers, terminal exposed via ttyd.
  • Sandbox preset — vendor-supplied pre-baked environment, no config.yml needed.
  • Multi-cloud — track that provisions resources in two different clouds.

Technique patterns

  • Pre-pull Docker images in setup — parallel docker pull calls in track-level setup to avoid live pull delays.
  • Bootstrap wait — until [ -f /opt/instruqt/bootstrap/host-bootstrap-completed ] loop at the top of track setup.
  • Idempotent binary wrapper — [ ! -f <backup> ] guard around mv when wrapping system binaries.
  • Heredoc-based project generation — generate a complete project tree via bash heredocs in setup, no external repo required.
  • Co-hosted portal plus API — serve portal and API from the same hostname and port, route by URL prefix.
  • Helper Python module — install a instruqt_tests.py module in dist-packages for shared check/solve logic.
  • Profile.d plus .bashrc dual-write — write env vars to both /etc/profile.d/*.sh and the user's .bashrc.
  • Non-root user pattern — useradd plus cmd: su - <user> on terminal tabs.
  • Custom CLI wrapper — Python script using httpx and rich, installed in PATH.
  • Companion code tab pair — editable starter file plus completed reference implementation as two code tabs.
  • Defensive challenge-level setup — query first, create only if absent. Survives skipped challenges.
  • agent variable cross-host hand-off — set on host A, read on host B via agent variable get.
  • CLOUDSDK auth credential override — scope GCP admin operations to a single command without polluting the user's gcloud session.
  • Granular check messaging — independent assertions, each with its own remediation message.
  • Scored certification rubric — point-based tally with color-coded scorecard.
  • Live polling dashboard — Flask or FastAPI app polling a database every few seconds; service tab.

Author patterns

  • Step-based body structure — numbered steps separated by horizontal rules; consistent colored headers.
  • Pre-challenge notes overlay — notes: slides displayed before challenge start, requiring enhanced_loading: false.
  • Tab buttons for inline navigation — [button](tab-N) references in the body to direct learners between tabs.
  • Variable interpolation in body — [[ Instruqt-Var key="..." hostname="..." ]] for dynamic values.
  • Agent-as-Teacher — for AI tool demos, structure challenges around prompts the learner pastes into an AI agent.
  • File-based quiz answers — for custom validation of multiple-choice content, write answer to a file and parse in check.

Tab Types and Their Quirks

The six assignment tab types, their key fields, and the most common gotcha for each.

terminal

Shell on a declared host.

- title: Terminal
  type: terminal
  hostname: workstation
  cmd: su - iggy   # optional
Gotcha

Without cmd:, the shell opens as root. Use cmd: su - <user> to drop into a non-root account.

service

HTTP server on a declared host port. Routes through Instruqt's proxy.

- title: Dashboard
  type: service
  hostname: workstation
  port: 5000
  path: /docs   # optional
Gotcha

Client-side fetch() calls must use relative paths only. The browser cannot resolve internal Docker hostnames.

code

File editor pointed at an absolute path on a declared host.

- title: Edit
  type: code
  hostname: workstation
  path: /root/app.py
Pattern

Pair an editable file with a second code tab pointing at a completed reference for a built-in hint.

browser

Cloud console browser tied to a virtualbrowsers block.

- title: AWS Console
  type: browser
  hostname: aws-console
Gotcha

Requires a matching virtualbrowsers entry in config.yml. Use this for provisioned cloud consoles, not arbitrary URLs.

external

Embedded URL frame.

- title: Vendor Site
  type: external
  url: https://docs.example.com

website

External URL tab.

- title: Public Docs
  type: website
  url: https://example.com
Gotcha

URL must be HTTPS. An http:// URL causes instruqt track push to fail validation.

Glossary of Instruqt Terms

Agent
The Instruqt component running on each host that handles platform communication, lifecycle scripts, and variable storage.
Assignment
The content of a single challenge, defined in assignment.md. Includes both YAML frontmatter (configuration) and Markdown body (text).
Challenge
One logical step in a track. A track contains one or more challenges, run in numbered order.
Check script
A lifecycle script that runs when the learner clicks the Check button. Validates the learner's work. Exit 0 means pass; exit 1 means fail.
Cleanup script
A lifecycle script that runs at the end of a challenge (challenge-level) or at the end of the track (track-level).
Cloud account block
A config.yml declaration of a provisioned cloud account or subscription: aws_accounts, azure_subscriptions, or gcp_projects.
Code tab
An assignment tab type that displays a file editor pointed at a path on a declared host.
Container
One of the three compute primitives. A Docker container running an Instruqt-provided base image.
Frontmatter
The YAML block at the top of an assignment.md file, between --- delimiters. Configures the challenge.
IAP tunnel
Identity-Aware Proxy tunnel, used to expose ports on private GCP VMs.
Instruqt-Var
A Markdown template tag for interpolating agent variable values into assignment bodies.
KasmVNC
A Docker image family that wraps a single GUI application and serves it over WebVNC. Used for browser-based GUI apps.
Lab session
One instance of a track running for one learner. Each lab session has its own provisioned infrastructure.
Lifecycle script
A bash script that runs at a specific point in a track's life: setup, check, solve, cleanup.
Lighter base image
Informal name for gcr.io/instruqt/shell, the minimal Instruqt container base image.
Cloud-client base image
Informal name for gcr.io/instruqt/cloud-client, the heavier Instruqt container base image with cloud CLIs preinstalled.
Sandbox preset
A pre-baked environment offered by the platform, usable instead of a custom config.yml.
Service tab
An assignment tab type that displays a web UI served from a declared host port, routed through Instruqt's traffic proxy.
Setup script
A lifecycle script that runs before a challenge or track becomes active.
Slug
A track or challenge's URL-safe identifier. Must match the directory name.
Solve script
A lifecycle script that puts a host into the state the corresponding check script accepts as a pass.
Track
The unit of work in Instruqt. A collection of infrastructure, challenges, and lifecycle scripts.
track.yml
The metadata file at the root of every track. Declares slug, ID, title, time limits, and lab UI configuration.
Virtual browser
A config.yml resource type that declares a browser tab pointing at a URL, often a cloud console.
Virtual machine (VM)
One of the three compute primitives. A full Linux VM running a stock or custom cloud image.
Workstation
Informal convention for the primary container or VM the learner interacts with through a terminal tab. Usually named workstation in config.yml.

A Closing Note

The book ends here, and the work begins.

If you have read this far, you have absorbed a lot of patterns, a lot of pitfalls, and a lot of opinion. Some of it will resonate immediately. Some of it will feel like overkill until the day it saves you. Some of it you will disagree with — and that is fine, as long as you have thought about it. The point of a field guide is not to settle every debate but to make the debates explicit so they can be had with care.

A few closing observations.

Tracks compound.

The first track you build is hard. The fifth is easier. The twentieth is fast. Almost every pattern in this book is something an author repeats across tracks, and the marginal cost of getting it right drops with every repetition. The marginal benefit — to the learners who run your tracks, to the colleagues who will inherit them, to the future you who will maintain them — keeps accumulating.

The platform rewards craft.

Instruqt does not impose taste. A perfectly functional track can be ugly, opaque, or inconsiderate, and the platform will still ship it. The craft is what distinguishes the tracks people complete and remember from the tracks they abandon and forget. Spend the small amounts of time on the small surfaces — the prompts, the loading messages, the failure messages — and the cumulative effect is real.

Your scars are valuable.

Every author who builds tracks long enough acquires a personal list of "things I will never do again." Write yours down. Share it with the next author. Add to it as new scars appear. The patterns in this book started as someone's scars; the patterns you discover next will start as yours.

The book is not exhaustive. It cannot be. The platform will evolve, the products you demonstrate will change, the audience will shift. New patterns will emerge; old ones will fall out of use. Treat the book as a snapshot — a useful one, but a snapshot.

Build well. Test honestly. Ship deliberately. Maintain with patience. The learners will not see any of it — and that is the goal.
